Make a computer see your hand

Turn on your webcam, hold up your hand, and watch the computer track every finger in real time. Then write a few simple rules that turn a thumbs up, a peace sign or a fist into a sound.

Try it

This is the finished project. Turn on your camera and try a thumbs up, a thumbs down, a peace sign, a fist, a pinch, or "rock on". By the end of this page you'll have written every rule behind it, and invented some of your own.

Your video stays on your computer. The hand-tracking model runs inside your browser. The page downloads the model once, and no video or picture is ever sent anywhere. No webcam? The demo hand works for every activity.

How a computer sees a hand

To a computer, a webcam frame is just a grid of numbers: a red, green and blue value for every pixel. Nothing in that grid says "this is a hand". So hand tracking happens in two steps, each done by a small neural network that was trained on many thousands of labeled hand photos:

  1. Camera frame640 × 480 pixels of color, about 30 times a second.
  2. Palm detectorFinds where in the frame any hands are, and draws a box around each one.
  3. Landmark modelLooks inside the box and predicts where 21 points on the hand are.
  4. 21 landmarksA list of points with x, y and z. This is all your code gets.

Neural network   Data

We're using Google's MediaPipe Hand Landmarker. Once a hand has been found, the tracker follows it from frame to frame instead of searching the whole picture again, which is why it's fast enough to run live in a browser.

The 21 landmarks

Every landmark is a point with three numbers:

x
Left to right across the picture: 0 at the left edge, 1 at the right edge.
y
Top to bottom: 0 at the top, 1 at the bottom. So a smaller y means higher up. This catches everyone at least once.
z
Depth, measured from the wrist. More negative means closer to the camera. It's a rough guess, much less reliable than x and y.

x and y are fractions of the picture, not pixels. That way the same rules work on any camera, at any resolution. The picture is mirrored like a selfie camera, so x matches what you see.

From points to fingers

        
The model gives you points, not fingers. "Is my index finger up?" is a question you answer, using geometry. Look at what happens to a finger when you bend it:

        
        
So the rule is: a finger is up when its tip is farther from the wrist than its middle joint. The site gives you a helper for that:

        
distance(a, b)   // how far apart two points are (uses x and y)

        
Why not just "the tip is higher than the middle joint"? That works only while your hand points straight up. Comparing distances to the wrist works with your hand tilted, sideways, or upside down.

Activity 4: Invent your own

        
Every gesture so far was a combination of up and down fingers. Two more tools open up a lot more:

        

          
Compare to hand size
Is the thumb touching the index finger? "Their distance is under 0.05" only works at one distance from the camera, because a closer hand is bigger. Divide by the hand's own size (wrist to middle knuckle) and the rule works anywhere: distance(p[4], p[8]) < 0.25 * handSize.

          
Remember the last frame
A variable declared outside detect keeps its value between frames. That lets you spot movement over time, like a wave.

          
Check order matters
detect returns at the first match. Put specific gestures (pinch) before general ones (open hand).

Bonus: Paint with your hand

        
So far your hand made sounds. It can also drive a shader: a tiny program that runs once for every pixel on the screen, every frame, on your graphics card. (That's the whole topic of SP-3. You don't need it to try this, but it helps.)

        
The shaders on this page get four extra inputs, filled in from the tracked hand every frame:

        

          
iHand[21]
The 21 landmarks, in pixels of the shader's picture. iHand[8].xy is your index fingertip. Careful: y points up here, like fragCoord, the opposite of the JavaScript activities.

          
iHandFound
1.0 while a hand is visible, fading to 0.0 when it's gone. Multiply by it so nothing glows with no hand around.

          
iHandSize
Wrist to middle knuckle, in pixels. The same hand-size trick from Activity 4.

          
iFingers[5]
1.0 if the thumb, index, middle, ring or pinky is up, using the same rule you wrote in Activity 2.

Where it breaks, and why

        
Rules like these are quick to write and easy to understand, but they're brittle. Try to break your own gestures and you'll find the same problems engineers deal with:

        
    
          
  • Hidden fingers. When fingers block each other, the model has to guess where they are. Its guesses can be confident and wrong.
    • 
          
  • Lighting and background. A dark room, strong backlight, or a background close to your skin tone makes the hand harder to find.
    • 
          
  • Edge cases. Is a half-bent finger up or down? Every threshold you pick will be wrong for somebody's hand.
    • 
          
  • Who it was trained on. A model only knows what it was shown. Teams test models across many skin tones, hand sizes and lighting conditions, because gaps in the training photos become gaps in what the model can see.
    • 
        

        
The next step past hand-written rules is to train a classifier: record a few hundred examples of each gesture's 21 landmarks and let a small model learn the boundaries for you. MediaPipe's Gesture Recognizer does exactly this, with a few gestures built in.
